this is what we discussed today in my reading group:

the main reading was a piece by claudia jones, written in 1949, about the disparities between white working-class and black working-class women at the time. in particular, she pointed out the gaps in wages, maternal mortality, lack of labor protections etc.[^1] i think something like this is ultimately interesting because i believe intersectionality is inseparable from class concerns. if you identify yourself by your demographic labels, you can systematically observe the special type of oppression you face as part of the working class.

for jones, being a woman in the united states meant that wages were lower because women's work was seen as a supplementary income in the nuclear family model (zetkin makes this point in her speech "only in conjunction with the proletarian woman will socialism be victorious"). in addition, being black meant that your people had gone from performing enslaved labor to wage labor that often looked the same - that didn't pay enough to support a family on a single income source. and as a black woman, you were paid less than half of what a white woman made, and again, the white woman was already being paid less than a white man. all while your wage was definitely not "supplementary" to your household.

the goal here is for each of us to unite and build solidarity around the fact that we, as a group, are continuously being exploited in every unique little way. even those we might deem "bourgeois" are still selling their labor for a wage. and if they took on debt in the hopes of moving up, part of that wage is expropriated right back through interest payments.

and we can't just stop at what is in front of us right now. it's not, "ok, we need to give black[/hispanic/immigrant] women more money, and then everything will be over." we need to keep the overarching goal in mind, because we have seen some moving-the-goalpost type of exploitation before: now, in a two-person heterosexual working-class household, instead of the woman performing the free, domestic labor of housework and cooking, that labor has been displaced onto gig workers. the same is true on an international level. if we somehow win workers' rights in the states, there will still be some form of exploitation the united states performs, like with latin america and the imf, where we pillage their resources.

i need to stress that this is a call to stay as informed as possible. speak to people in different communities to gain perspective and show solidarity with each other. you can make individual choices each day to contribute or to feel good, and i do believe in collective action, but i do not believe that the most effective form of collective action today looks like each of us, as consumers, taking power by opting in and out of what we pay for.

take, for example, someone who buys a free palestine shirt that is made by a corporation that donates money to humanitarian aid. at the end of the day, israel is restricting humanitarian aid at the rafah border so much of it never reaches palestinians. the onus is not on the individual to make surface-level, seemingly "more moral" choices; this is how the ruling class can neutralize us.[^2]

we need to unionize, spread the message, and build solidarity with one another. if you want to participate in actions now, you can join court watches, [break the chain](https://wecanbreakthechain.com/), or support [no more 24](https://nomore24.org/). there are many immediate actions we can take to help those who are marginalized, but the most beneficial thing we can do to set ourselves up for the future is to be aligned and working toward one collective cause.

[^1]: at the time, the majority of black women made less than half of what white women did. black women also relied on that income more: two out of five black women worked for a living, compared to two out of eight white women, largely because black men were paid so little. most were stuck in domestic work, which was excluded from social security and most minimum-wage laws, so only about one in ten black women workers had minimum-wage protection. and the maternity death rate for black women was triple that of white women. i don't imagine current day figures demonstrate that this gap has been closed fully - even on the note of labor protections for domestic workers, the fight is ongoing and looks different today ([no more 24](https://nomore24.org/)).

[^2]: a friend and i discussed this topic a little more and the conclusion we came to for now is that consumer choices are still a net positive, regardless of how much they actually affect the structures in place. even if they don't move the needle, they're an act of solidarity. we cannot let perfectionism immobilize us, but we have to keep in mind that these choices alone are not solutions and, when treated as such, they play into the hands of the ruling class.
